About

I had the same questions
this job answers.

I started the MS Information Systems - Bridge program on the Seattle campus this January. I got here the way a lot of COE applicants do: from another country, and from a non-technical background. Before this I spent a year as a student ambassador in the engineering faculty at the University of Nottingham.

That job was pretty much this one. I wrote and kept up a 206-question guide in two languages, covering nine topics, for a whole year. I helped more than 100 students — some still applying, some already admitted — in an online group and in evening office hours. Every month I sorted through what people were asking and passed it to my manager. I also handled the practical side of campus tours, student panels, an online Q&A series and social events: booking rooms, setting dates, chasing people, following up afterwards.

And it isn't all in the past. Since September I've been an Adobe Student Ambassador here, making and posting content for Northeastern students while following Adobe's rules for how their brand is used. Both jobs taught me the same thing: if you speak for an organisation, be useful to the reader first and sell second.

Adobe Student Ambassador,
Northeastern Seattle.

Northeastern already pays for Adobe Express, and every student can use it for free. Most students have never opened it. My first real post as an ambassador was a five-slide LinkedIn carousel about three things students actually have to do: the slides due tomorrow, the resume for internship season, and the poster for a club event.

I picked the topic, wrote the text, and designed all five slides in Adobe Express, following Adobe's brand rules. The last slide is a QR code that takes you straight to the free license. The post is labelled as sponsored, because that's the rule and because a hidden ad works worse anyway.

This job needs the same thing. The resource was already there — people just didn't know about it. Fixing that is a writing and design problem.
